Why did my stock fall?

In short

A falling price means sellers outnumbered buyers that day, nothing more. The usual suspects are profit-taking after a rally, disappointing results, going ex-dividend, or just a bad day for the whole market.

You did not necessarily do anything wrong

Prices move because buyers and sellers disagree, not because someone made a mistake. A stock can fall on a day the company did everything right, simply because more holders wanted to sell than buyers wanted to buy.

Before worrying, check how far it fell and whether the whole market fell too. A 1% dip on a red market day is noise. A 10% crash on good market breadth deserves a closer look.

The five usual suspects

1. Profit-taking. After a strong rally, early buyers sell to lock in gains. This is healthy and extremely common.

2. Bad or disappointing results. Quarterly earnings missed expectations, or guidance was cut. Check the earnings dashboard for the dates.

3. Ex-dividend. On the ex-div date the price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount, because new buyers no longer get the payout. Your loss on price is offset by the cash dividend coming your way.

4. Sector or market rotation. Money moves between sectors. Banks can fall while oil stocks rally, and vice versa, with no company-specific news at all.

5. News and sentiment. Regulatory fines, management changes, downgrades, or just fear. Read beyond the headline before acting.

What to do instead of panicking

Ask three questions: did the reason I bought change? If you bought for a 5-year dividend story, a 3% dip changes nothing. Is the whole market down? Then it is probably not about your stock. Would I buy more at this price? If yes, the fall is an opportunity; if the thought terrifies you, your position may be too big.

Never sell in the first hour of panic. Prices are most emotional at the open. Give it a day, read the news, then decide with a clear head.
